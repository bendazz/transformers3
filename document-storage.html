Document Storage

Store documents in a vector database and search them with natural language

1 From Comparing to Storing

So far, you've seen how text gets converted into vectors (embeddings) and how cosine similarity measures how related two pieces of text are. But comparing two texts at a time isn't very useful in the real world.

A vector database lets you store many documents, each with its embedding. Then when you have a question, the database embeds your question and finds the stored documents whose vectors are closest — all in one step.

On this page, we'll build up a collection of documents and search it. The same embedding model from the previous tutorial runs in your browser — no server needed.

Type a sentence and click Add. The embedding model will convert your text into a 384-dimensional vector and store both the text and its vector in the collection.

4 Search

Now type a question or phrase. The model embeds your query using the same model, then compares it against every stored document's vector. Results are ranked by distance — lower distance means the vectors are closer together (more similar).

Try it: Search for something related to your documents but worded differently. For example, if you stored "The cat curled up on the warm windowsill", try searching "a pet relaxing at home." The model understands that these mean similar things even though they share no words in common.

5 What Just Happened

You just did exactly what a vector database like ChromaDB does. Here's the breakdown:

When you...The database...
Added a document Ran it through the embedding model to get a 384-dim vector, then stored both the text and the vector
Searched with a query Embedded the query with the same model, then computed the distance to every stored vector
Saw ranked results Sorted all documents by distance and returned the closest ones
Key insight: You never told the database which documents are about animals or weather or cooking. The embedding model figured out the meaning on its own and placed similar documents near each other in 384-dimensional space. That's the whole point of embeddings — they capture meaning, not just keywords.

6 Looking Ahead

This tutorial stored short, single sentences. But real-world documents are much longer — articles, emails, book chapters. What happens when a document is too long for the embedding model to handle?

That's where chunking comes in: splitting long documents into smaller pieces so each piece can be embedded and searched independently. But that's a topic for the next tutorial.
